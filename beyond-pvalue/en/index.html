The p-value is subject to numerous errors, misinterpretations, mistaken beliefs, and cognitive biases. This section aims to demystify the most common pitfalls, dispel misconceptions, and clarify what the p-value is and what it is not.

The p-value is not the probability of the null hypothesis $H_0$

Believing that the p-value represents the probability that the null hypothesis is true given what has been observed is arguably the most common error — and the most human one. In fact, it is natural, rather logical, and is precisely what one would want to know and what our brain demands during an experiment. If one has an initial belief about the world around us ($H_0$) and observes data (the result of the experiment), the most instinctive question is indeed: “ In light of this result, what is the probability that my initial hypothesis is true? ” But unfortunately, in the context of the p-value, mathematics does not allow us to answer this question. It closes this door on us, but hands us the keys to another: “ What is the probability of observing a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming that my initial hypothesis is true? ” It is subtle, but that is precisely what the p-value is — nothing more, nothing less.

Frustrating, but that is how it is! There is indeed a way of calculating the probability that $H_0$ is true given the observed data — but it requires an additional ingredient: being able to quantify how strongly one believes in $H_0$ before even collecting the data. Yet in experimental sciences in particular, this initial belief is very often impossible to evaluate objectively — and without it, there is no way of going further. This approach does nonetheless exist (because yes, mathematics is remarkably well-designed), and it rests on the magnificent Bayes' theorem, which proves extraordinarily powerful in certain contexts: it is this theorem that allows one to turn the problem on its head and evaluate the probability that $H_0$ is true given the observed data — but that is a story for another time...

To summarise: in an ideal world, one would calculate $\mathbb{P}(H_0 \mid \text{data})$ — the probability that our initial hypothesis, our initial belief, is true in light of what has been observed. This is exactly what our brain demands, and it would be far more satisfying. But experimental sciences condemn us to calculating something subtly different: $\mathbb{P}(\text{data at least as extreme} \mid H_0)$ — the probability of observing results as extreme as our own, assuming that $H_0$, our initial belief, is true. These are not the same thing, and as we saw in our introduction to probabilities, swapping these two quantities is a fundamental error. The p-value is a powerful tool — provided one never loses sight of what it truly measures, and what it does not.

Proof by contradiction

Calculating a p-value is in a sense a proof by contradiction — a technique that is used far more often than one might think in everyday life. The principle is simple: one assumes something to be true, and checks whether what is observed is consistent with this assumption. If the observations fit rather well, one retains the initial hypothesis — not because it is necessarily true, but because there is no good reason to call it into question; if the data contradict it too plainly, the initial hypothesis no longer holds: one must let it go.

Imagine you are baking a cake for your child's birthday and you leave it to cool on the table whilst stepping out of the room for a few minutes. When you return, the cake is no longer whole. You assume that your dog Laska, an adorable little Australian shepherd, is the culprit — this is your $H_0$. From there, you check whether the data fit this hypothesis, and that is the only thing you can do: does the missing portion appear to have been cut with a knife, or torn apart by the teeth of a hungry dog?

The edges are irregular and jagged, with teeth marks: the data are therefore consistent with $H_0$ — Laska is not known for her mastery of cutlery. On the other hand, if the cut had been clean and straight, the data would have contradicted $H_0$ and suspicion would have naturally fallen on the child. That is proof by contradiction: a contradiction that allows one to refute the initial hypothesis. Of course, one can never be 100% certain — perhaps the child deliberately nibbled at the edges or avoided using a knife to frame the dog. But here the data make certain hypotheses far more credible than others. And that is precisely the reasoning the p-value carries out in practice: it does not answer the question "What is the probability that Laska ate the slice of cake given that there are teeth marks?" — that would be too good to be true. It answers the question "If Laska had eaten the slice, what is the probability of observing such a cut with teeth marks?" It is subtle, and that is where the whole difficulty lies: one never calculates the probability that $H_0$ is true, only the likelihood of the data under $H_0$. Two questions that look alike, but do not mean the same thing at all.

$$\text{p-value} := \mathbb{P}(H_0 \mid \text{data})$$
$$\text{p-value} := \mathbb{P}(\text{data} \mid H_0 \text{ true})$$

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence

Another very widespread belief is that if $H_0$ is not rejected (p ≥ 0.05), then it must necessarily be true. In the context of scientific studies, one will often hear — wrongly — that there is no difference between two groups because p ≥ 0.05. In reality, when the null hypothesis $H_0$ is not rejected, there are indeed two possible scenarios: either it is genuinely true, or it is false but we have not gathered sufficient evidence to demonstrate it.

To bear this in mind, let us revisit the metaphor of extraterrestrial detection introduced in the section “ Introduction to the p-value ”. If one defines $H_0$ as “ Extraterrestrials do not exist ” and one indeed observes no extraterrestrials during an experiment, it would be rash and wrong to claim with certainty that they do not exist. Two possibilities remain: either they truly do not exist, or they do exist but we do not yet have proof of it. The correct way to conclude is to say “ The experiment did not allow us to reject $H_0$ ” or “ The data do not provide sufficient evidence to reject $H_0$ ”. Thus, one must remain in the world of $H_0$, the one where extraterrestrials do not exist, but this does not guarantee that this conclusion is correct, nor that the world of $H_1$ does not exist.

That said, there are situations where one has very good reasons — theoretical or experimental — to believe that $H_0$ is genuinely true when p ≥ 0.05. Such a p-value therefore confirms the null hypothesis, our initial belief. In this case, it becomes reasonable and legitimate to conclude that there is no difference between two groups, for instance, or that extraterrestrials do not exist. But bear in mind: it is not the p-value that allows this conclusion — it is the broader scientific context that permits it.

When $H_0$ is not rejected, it is therefore essential to place this result in its scientific context: is it because $H_0$ is genuinely true, or simply because the data are not sufficient to contradict it? A sample that is too small, variability that is too great, an ill-suited test — all reasons that can lead to failing to reject $H_0$, not because it is true, but because the evidence is lacking. At that point, it is the state of established knowledge that allows one to settle the matter.

The presumption of innocence

The p-value reasoning is comparable to the presumption of innocence: one always assumes that $H_0$ is initially true (the person is innocent) until proven otherwise. When a person is accused, they are a priori innocent; and sufficient evidence must be accumulated in order to declare them guilty (shifting from $H_0$ to $H_1$). In many cases, certain matters are “ dropped ”: this does absolutely not mean that the person is innocent, but simply that the evidence is not sufficient to establish their guilt. When a case is dropped, either the person is innocent, or they are guilty but the evidence is lacking.

To summarise: a dropped case is therefore not equivalent to a declaration of innocence, or in other words: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence

p = 0.049 is not radically different from p = 0.051

One might think that p = 0.051 and p = 0.049 are worlds apart. This is indeed the case to a certain extent, since the former leads us to remain in the world of $H_0$ whilst the latter allows us to shift towards that of $H_1$: at the end of the experiment, we are indeed in two diametrically opposed worlds. Yet one must be fully aware that these two numbers remain probabilities and therefore exist on a continuum: a probability (including the p-value) as such is not a switch that allows one to toggle between the worlds $H_0$ and $H_1$ — it is simply a measure of the likelihood of a result in a given world. It is therefore difficult to argue that an event with a 4.9% chance of occurring is fundamentally different from one with a 5.1% chance. I think we can all agree that such events are equally likely.

It is us, human beings — well, neither you nor I —, who decided to draw a boundary between two probabilities. We have transformed an objective measure (a probability) into a subjective binary conclusion: which world are we in? Yet this boundary is by definition subjective, as it is set arbitrarily and rests on no law of nature. Throughout our journey, we have used a threshold of 0.05 to move from $H_0$ to $H_1$, but this choice was indeed an arbitrary convention: any other value could serve as the boundary between the ordinary and the extraordinary. In disciplines that rely on the p-value to draw conclusions, 0.05 is the most widespread convention — but it is not uncommon to encounter studies that adopt a stricter threshold, such as 0.01, when the stakes demand it.

The choice of the tipping point between the two worlds $H_0$ and $H_1$ is not trivial: all the conclusions we have drawn so far rest on the convention of 0.05. Changing this tipping point means changing the rules of the game — and therefore potentially changing the conclusions. A result with p = 0.03 is extraordinary with a tipping point of 0.05, but no longer is if it is moved to 0.01. Conversely, a result with p = 0.08 is ordinary at 0.05 but would become extraordinary if 0.10 were chosen as the tipping point. This is why it is essential to always specify the chosen tipping point before conducting an analysis — and above all not to choose it after having looked at the data, which would amount to moving the finish line once the race is over. To illustrate this with the examples encountered along our journey: with a boundary of 0.01, our conclusions collapse. The coin we had declared biased no longer is — at least, we no longer have proof of it. And the tree we had identified as a beech can no longer be considered as such. All of this, without the data having changed one iota!

When one seeks to shift towards the world of $H_1$ and obtains a p-value of 0.051, the situation can be frustrating if 0.05 had been chosen as the tipping point: we were only 0.002 away from being able to shift, and yet we are compelled to remain in $H_0$. Faced with this situation, the temptation is great to conclude that the world of $H_1$ is “ almost ” true. In the context of statistical tests, one may hear expressions such as “ there is a trend ”. In both cases, these expressions all convey the same reality: we were truly not far from shifting towards $H_1$ — but we did not shift. And in statistics, almost does not count: the boundary has not been crossed and we must remain in $H_0$, whether we like it or not.

Actually, saying that we were not far from shifting towards $H_1$ is factually true but does not change much. The threshold of 0.05 was defined precisely to make things binary: either one is in $H_0$, or one is in $H_1$. There is unfortunately no in-between, no “ almost $H_1$ ”. This is moreover one of the fundamental criticisms that can be levelled at the p-value: this binary nature is brutal, and it has no truly satisfactory solution to this day.

The sudden darkness bias

The sudden darkness bias is the bias that leads us to perceive as a sharp break what is in reality a continuous transition. In practice, night never falls all at once: the light fades gradually, dusk sets in, then darkness gains ground little by little, without any truly instantaneous shift. Yet at some point, one is compelled to set a threshold and say “ it is dark ”. And yet, the brightness a few moments before and a few moments after this threshold is in reality almost the same.

This problem is far more universal than it might seem and arises whenever humans impose an arbitrary threshold on a continuous objective measure. Let us take the example of the age of majority: in France, it is set at 18. Thus, a person aged 17 years and 11 months is a minor, but a person aged 18 years and 1 month is an adult. Worse still: a person aged 17 years, 11 months, and 364 days is a minor, but a person aged 18 years, 0 months, and 1 day is an adult. The gap between the two? 48 hours. Yet one can vote, get a tattoo, drive, sign a contract — the other cannot. Thus, saying that a person aged 17 years, 11 months, and 364 days is “ almost an adult ” is factually true, but opens no doors for them: in regulatory matters, as with the p-value, almost does not count.

Everyday life is full of examples built on this effect. Scoring 9.99/20 or 10.01/20 in the baccalauréat: factually, both pupils are at the same academic level — yet one leaves with the diploma, the other does not. Luggage on a plane: a suitcase weighing 22.9 kg theoretically costs nothing extra, whilst one weighing 23.1 kg may incur additional charges. Yet these extra 200 grams change virtually nothing about the flight: neither fuel consumption, nor safety, nor the comfort of the other passengers are perceptibly affected. In all these cases, the boundary is a practical necessity because a decision must be made, a choice taken. But reality itself cares nothing for our compromises: it is continuous, infinitely more nuanced than the categories we impose upon it, and is utterly indifferent to our arbitrary thresholds, which are nothing but conventions, not truths. One must never forget that they are our invention, not nature's. That is the price to pay for turning a complex world into simple decisions.
